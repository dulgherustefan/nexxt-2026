# AI agents, ML and workflow automation in bank internal operations (2024–2026): process benchmarks, case studies, analyst views, Romanian vendors, EU/RO regulation, failure modes and safeguards

Research date: 28 October 2026. Context: NEXXT Summit 2026, Raiffeisen track "AI for Operational Efficiency". Source labels used below: **[vendor]** means a vendor marketing claim, **[bank self-reported]** means a company announcement, **[secondary]** means I found the figure only in an aggregator or press summary and could not open the primary source, **[independent]** means a regulator, academic or court source.

---

## Q1. Per-process benchmarks: volumes, manual effort, cost per case, error/false-positive rates, and what AI or agents have achieved

### Takeaway
Every candidate process has a well-documented baseline problem that a team can cite: about 90–95%+ false positives in AML transaction monitoring, about 85–99% in sanctions screening, corporate KYC reviews costing about $1.5–3.5k and taking about 60–150 days, 1–5% of payments needing a manual investigation, and 60–75% of letter-of-credit presentations being discrepant. Reported AI results are large (alert handling cut from 15–30 minutes to seconds, document extraction from 30 minutes to 30 seconds, auto-match rates of 75–95%), but almost all of them come from banks or vendors, and I found almost no independent before-and-after evidence. Baselines are easier to defend than claimed uplifts.

### Cited Findings

**AML transaction-monitoring alert triage and investigation**
- A NICE Actimize industry survey of Tier 1, mid-size and sub-$10bn banks found that "almost 95 percent of alerts" result in false positives, with some institutions reporting more (the report is undated in the search results) — [NICE Actimize, AI in AML insights report](https://www.niceactimize.com/Documents/aml_ai_in_aml_insights_report.pdf)
- McKinsey is widely quoted as saying transaction-monitoring false positives exceed 90% at most banks and 98% for some customer-risk and monitoring models. **[secondary]**: these are vendor pages citing McKinsey; I did not see the McKinsey original — [Fluxforce](https://www.fluxforce.ai/answers/what-percentage-of-aml-alerts-are-false-positives)
- Starling Bank (UK) reports that its in-house agentic investigator cut investigation time per alert from a typical 15–30 minutes for a human to under 17 seconds. **[bank self-reported]**, relayed via AML Intelligence — [Sterling Finance summary](https://sterlingfinance.uk/news/starling-bank-launches-native-ai-agent-to-automate-aml-investigations)
- The FIS AML investigations agent, built with Anthropic, is projected by FIS to cut Level-1 alert review time by more than 90% and Level-2 case investigation time by about 98%. BMO is among the first deployers. These are projections, not measured results **[vendor]** — [The Paypers](https://thepaypers.com/fraud-and-fincrime/news/fis-opens-aml-investigations-agent-built-with-anthropic-to-us-banks)
- BMO reportedly cut false AML alerts by about 10% and adverse-media searches from about 180 to about 20 minutes. Santander's Openbank models reportedly process about 100,000 alerts a year and finish in minutes investigations that used to take hours. **[secondary]**: from a research roundup, and many of these are ML rather than agentic deployments — [Neurons Lab roundup 2026](https://neurons-lab.com/articles/agentic-ai-in-financial-services-2026/)
- McKinsey (2025) describes a "productivity ladder" for agentic AI in financial-crime work. In a secondary summary of McKinsey's estimates, earlier gen-AI tools gave about 20% productivity gains, while agentic workflows could give 200–2,000% when one compliance officer supervises about 20 agents. **[secondary]**: I could not open the McKinsey page because both fetch attempts timed out — [RapidFlow summary](https://www.rapidflowapps.com/news/mckinsey-agentic-ai-could-lift-bank-compliance-productivity-up-to-20x-far-beyond-earlier-ai-tools/); primary: [McKinsey, "Agentic AI in financial-crime prevention: Lessons from winners"](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/risk-and-resilience/our-insights/agentic-ai-in-financial-crime-prevention-lessons-from-winners)

**KYC onboarding and periodic review**
- Fenergo's survey of corporate and institutional banks puts the cost of a manual corporate KYC review at $1,501–3,500. Interexy cautions that this corporate figure is often wrongly retold as a retail per-customer cost — [Interexy](https://interexy.com/kyc-cost-per-customer)
- Fenergo's 2024 figure is an average of $2,274 per corporate/commercial client review, down 12% from $2,598 in 2023. Secondary sources describe the survey sample inconsistently (453 executives vs "1000+") — [Sphinx](https://sphinxhq.com/blog-posts/cost-of-manual-kyb-workflows)
- Fenergo also reports that more than half of financial institutions spend 61–150 days on client KYC reviews, at an average of about $2,200 per review — [Fenergo blog on perpetual KYC](https://www.fenergo.com/blogs/perpetual-kyc-pkyc)
- At US investment banks, Fenergo found a mean of 82 days to onboard each client, down from 117 days the year before — [Fenergo newsroom](https://resources.fenergo.com/newsroom/fenergo-kyc-trends-report-us-investment-banks-navigate-intensified-regulatory-reform)
- ING's GenAI solution for wholesale customer-due-diligence data extraction reached "nearly 90% accuracy", and ING projects at least a 50% cut in average handling time (the accuracy is demonstrated, the handling time is projected) **[bank self-reported]** — [Evident Insights](https://evidentinsights.com/insights/articles/ing-genai-in-wholesale-banking)
- ING's Marnix van Stiphout said CDD now takes seconds, compared with a day in good cases and weeks in bad ones. He also said 70–80 of the roughly 100 questions in a typical CDD file can be answered from data the bank already holds — [McKinsey interview with ING](https://mckinsey.com/industries/financial-services/our-insights/deploying-ai-at-speed-and-scale-talking-with-ings-marnix-van-stiphout)
- ING's KYC agent saved relationship managers an average of 45 minutes per legal-entity onboarding. ING plans to give the suite to RMs in the Netherlands and Belgium by the end of 2026 **[bank self-reported, small sample]** — [WealthBriefing](https://www.wealthbriefing.com/html/article.php/ing-uses-ai-to-give-relationship-managers-more-time-for-clients-)

**Sanctions and name-screening hits**
- A 2024 peer-reviewed paper says sanctions-screening false-positive rates "exceed 90%". It also finds that NLP matching lowers false negatives but produces more false-positive alerts **[independent]** — [Frontiers in AI, 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/artificial-intelligence/articles/10.3389/frai.2024.1374323/pdf)
- Up to 99% of sanctions-screening alerts in cross-border payments are false positives (commentary piece) — [Retail Banker International](https://www.retailbankerinternational.com/comment/why-99-percent-sanctions-screening-alerts-false-positives/)
- "Up to 97%" of sanctions alerts are false positives **[vendor]** — [SymphonyAI guide](https://www.symphonyai.com/wp-content/uploads/SymphonyAI-Financial-Guide-to-AI-for-Sanctions-Screening.pdf)

**Payment exceptions, repairs and investigations**
- The industry cost of payment exceptions and investigations (E&I) is most often given as about $1.6bn a year, a Swift estimate repeated by vendors. Swift's E&I Transformation Report, as cited by Bottomline, says free-text MT messages make up more than 72% of E&I communications **[secondary]** — [Bottomline](https://www.bottomline.com/resources/blog/iso-20022-enabled-customer-case-management-final-hurdle-overcome-achieving-payments-modernisation); [Evonsys](https://www.evonsys.com/tracei-blogs/payment-investigations-migrating-to-iso-20022-how-data-rich-messages-reshape-workflows)
- Estimates of the share of payments that need an investigation range from 1–3% to 2–5% depending on the source. Validata says "3%+". Swift-attributed resolution times are 5–10 days (about 6+ working days on average) **[vendor/secondary]** — [Validata](https://www.validata-software.com/solutions/payments-investigation-and-case-manager/); [Evonsys](https://www.evonsys.com/tracei-blogs/iso-20022-and-the-future-of-exception-handling-why-this-migration-is-a-defining-moment-for-banks)
- NatWest's illustrative ISO 20022 example uses about £30 per bank investigation case and about £5 to handle each manual exception. These are hypothetical figures, not a benchmark — [NatWest](https://www.natwest.com/corporates/insights/regulation/iso-20022-for-finance-teams.html)
- Smart Stream claims its platform cuts E&I resolution costs by "up to 70%" **[vendor]** — [Smart Stream](https://smart.stream/solutions/smart-payments/)
- Deutsche Bank published a 2025 corporate guide to ISO 20022 exceptions and investigations, which is useful for structured E&I message design — [DB Guide to ISO 20022 E&I (2025)](https://flow.db.com/files/documents/more/publications/white-papers-guides/2025/DB-Guide-to-ISO20022-EI-WP-Web-Secured.pdf)

**Reconciliation (nostro, card settlement, GL, bank-statement matching)**
- Vendor-reported AI auto-match rates mostly fall between 75% and 95%. One compilation reports BlackLine 2025 benchmarks of 91% average auto-match for deployments live more than 12 months but 78% for those live under 6 months, and Trintech Cadency at 93% for high-volume and 84% for low-volume accounts. AutoRek/Howden reports 76% automatic matching with processing time more than halved. A NetSuite customer went from 10–15 hours to under 1 hour per bank account at about 95% auto-match **[vendor, compiled by secondary pages, primaries not opened]** — [Stealth Agents statistics 2026](https://stealthagents.com/research/ai-bank-reconciliation-automation-statistics-2026); [Houseblend guide](https://www.houseblend.io/articles/pdfs/netsuite-ai-bank-matching-reconciliation-guide-b646a06adb2d53e2.pdf); [AutoRek profile](https://yespress.io/autorek)
- Practitioner guidance: auto-match rate alone can mislead because automation clears the easy items and hides a growing pile of hard ones. Teams should also track the count and age of open exceptions and the false-match rate. One vendor uses a tiered design in which ML generates and ranks candidates while deterministic rules keep the final auto-match decision — [LearnSignal guide](https://learnsignal.com/blog/ai-for-account-reconciliations); [Digiqt nostro agent page](https://digiqt.com/ai-agent/financial-services/reconciliation/nostro-reconciliation-ai-agent-for-reconciliation-in-financial-services/)
- NeoXam's ML exception classification and remediation was used in a global institution's high-volume credit-card business (the excerpt gave no numbers) **[vendor]** — [NeoXam case study](https://www.neoxam.com/clients/machine-learning-reconciliation/)
- At Banco Azteca, the planned next steps for UiPath Maestro orchestration include ATM cash monitoring and AML (see Q2) — [FF News](https://ffnews.com/news/banco-azteca-evolves-from-automation-to-enterprise-scale-business-orchestration--e2665628)

**Card disputes and chargebacks**
- Banks spend an estimated $25–50 per dispute on manual investigation, and resolution takes days or weeks. The same vendor claims automation brings this to $2–5 per dispute **[vendor]** — [Maven AGI, banking transaction disputes](https://www.mavenagi.com/resources/banking-transaction-disputes)
- Merchant-side benchmarks attributed to Chargebacks911 (2025) put internal labour at about $28 per manually worked dispute (range $10–70), total cost per dispute at about $166, and 60–80% of chargebacks as some form of friendly fraud. These figures are mainly merchant-side, not issuer-side **[vendor/secondary]** — [Chargeflow, manual vs automated chargeback costs](https://www.chargeflow.io/blog/cost-of-manual-vs-automated-chargeback-processing)

**Garnishment and legal-order processing (Romania: "popriri")**
- In December 2020 BCR began rolling out AI/ML automation of garnishment document processing across 371 branches, using a solution from Zipper România. Data is extracted automatically, and responses to bailiffs (executori) are generated and sent electronically. The vendor claims the system removes human error **[vendor/bank, 2020, dated]** — [Vocea Olteniei](https://www.vocea-olteniei.ro/2020/12/28/automatizarea-procesarii-documentelor-de-popriri-la-bcr/)
- ANAF's e-Popriri system (OPANAF 878/2022) automates setting up and lifting bank garnishments. ANAF has proposed amendments after institutions transferred amounts without knowing what other banks had already transferred. Under the framework, confirmed amounts are ordered by value in descending order — [CECCAR Business Magazine](https://www.ceccarbusinessmagazine.ro/anaf-propune-modificari-la-mecanismul-privind-automatizarea-infiintarii-si-ridicarii-popririlor-bancare/s/k8jVKT9MVfb048GOitqF); [OPANAF 878/2022](https://static.anaf.ro/static/10/Anaf/legislatie/OPANAF_878_2022.pdf)
- For banks in the ANAF protocol, garnishments are set up and lifted automatically, and they cannot be lifted at the client's request — [Revolut RO disclosure on garnishments](https://www.revolut.com/ro-RO/legal/informatii-popriri/)
- A 2026 legal commentary notes that an automatic electronic garnishment blocks an account within seconds, while reversing an error needs human validation by the bank, the bailiff and ANAF and can take days or weeks. It also describes an accountability gap: the bank points to the e-Popriri order, the tax authority to aggregated data, and the bailiff to its software output — [JURIDICE.ro](https://www.juridice.ro/862896/interoperabilitatea-bazelor-de-date-in-procedura-popririi-electronice-de-la-automatizarea-interogarilor-la-riscul-erorilor-de-sistem.html)
- Matching is done by CNP (personal numeric code), so protected income such as child allowances can be blocked, and the account holder must ask for protected sums to be filtered out and provide proof — [InfoContact](https://www.infocontact.ro/cont-blocat-abuziv-anaf-executor/)

**Trade-finance document checking (letters of credit, UCP 600)**
- The most-cited anchor is the ICC's statement, made when introducing UCP 600, that about 70% of documents presented under LCs were rejected on first presentation. That figure is about two decades old, and an earlier 2005 survey put the average at 56%. Current vendor estimates range from 60% to 80% — [FloridAI analysis](https://floridai.agency/blog/letter-of-credit-discrepancy-rate-ai); [Seasaw](https://seasaw.ai/resources/lc-document-discrepancies/); [Kanan Labs](https://kananlabs.in/publications/lc-first-presentation-discrepancies/)
- Most discrepancies are clerical: date-format mismatches between the bill of lading and the invoice, port-name variants, and amount tolerances. An ICC-based breakdown attributes 38% to transport documents, 27% to commercial invoices and 19% to insurance documents **[secondary]** — [FloridAI](https://floridai.agency/blog/letter-of-credit-discrepancy-rate-ai); [Seasaw](https://seasaw.ai/resources/lc-document-discrepancies/)
- Automated review handles deterministic field checks well but "cannot adjudicate judgment calls under UCP 600". Vendor detection claims such as 99.4% have not been independently verified — [FloridAI](https://floridai.agency/blog/letter-of-credit-discrepancy-rate-ai)

### Inferences
- The strongest business-value argument uses a well-sourced baseline (95% false positives, 60–75% discrepancies, $25–50 per manual dispute, ~$2.2k per corporate KYC review) and a conservative uplift range, rather than vendor headline claims (">90%", "98%").
- Popriri (garnishment) processing is a distinctively Romanian, high-volume, rules-plus-documents process. BCR has run AI extraction on it since 2020, and the e-Popriri error-reversal and protected-income issues are public. It fits the track's "documents + data + predefined rules" brief well, and the edge cases (multiple banks, protected income, wrong CNP match, partial amounts) give the agent real exceptions to handle, which the jury scores at 25%.
- Payment investigations and reconciliation exceptions are the other natural fits: the input is structured (ISO 20022 / MT, statements) but messy (more than 72% free text in E&I), the rules are clear, and the main KPIs (auto-match rate, false-match rate, exception ageing, cost per case) are already defined by the industry.
- For AML or sanctions triage, a credible hackathon scope is "triage, summarise and draft the disposition rationale with a human decision", not "auto-close alerts". Every bank case found (Starling, FIS/BMO, ING) keeps humans in the decision.

### Gaps
- No independent, audited before-and-after data was found for any AI deployment in these processes. Nearly all uplift figures are bank or vendor self-reported.
- No Romanian volume data was found for popriri per bank per year, or for complaints, disputes or payment investigations at Romanian banks. These could be requested from Raiffeisen mentors during the hackathon.
- No reliable issuer-side (bank) cost per chargeback for the EU or Romania was found. Most figures are US and merchant-side.
- No primary McKinsey, PwC or Everest Group source was opened for the AML false-positive and alert-to-SAR ratios. An EY figure (agentic AI giving a 50% time reduction, about 2 hours per AML investigation) appeared only in search snippets without a traceable primary source, so I left it out.
- No credible benchmark was found for ATM cash reconciliation or e-invoice (RO e-Factura) versus payment matching at banks.

## Q2. Concrete 2023–2026 case studies of GenAI or agentic AI in bank operations, with numbers (global and CEE/Romania)

### Takeaway
Large banks report enterprise-wide GenAI rollouts with modest, self-reported productivity per person (BBVA about 3 hours/week, Lloyds £50m of value in 2025, JPMorgan about $2bn/yr of benefit roughly matching its spend) and are now moving to agents inside specific processes (ING KYC, Starling AML, Citi Stylus agents, Commerzbank process agents). Romanian banks are earlier: internal knowledge assistants (Raiffeisen's ARI, January 2024), document automation (BCR popriri), conversational AI (Alpha Bank's Dana, BT/Druid), and BT's first agent-initiated payment (July 2026). None has published hard operational metrics.

## Q5. Regulatory constraints in EU and Romanian banks: EU AI Act (high-risk scope, obligations, timeline after the Digital Omnibus), GDPR Art. 22, DORA, and EBA / ECB / BNR statements

### Takeaway
As of 28 October 2026, the AI Act Digital Omnibus is law: Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force since 27 July 2026. It moves Annex III high-risk obligations, which include creditworthiness assessment and credit scoring of natural persons, from 2 August 2026 to **2 December 2027**, and Annex I to 2 August 2028, with fixed dates and no standards-linked trigger. Most internal operations agents (reconciliation, payment investigations, AML/sanctions triage, popriri, complaint triage) are not Annex III high-risk. Loan-application validation becomes high-risk only if the agent evaluates creditworthiness or profiles natural persons. Even outside the AI Act, GDPR Art. 22 (strengthened by CJEU SCHUFA), DORA's third-party ICT risk rules (applicable since 17 January 2025) and supervisors' focus on governance and cyber set the design constraints.

### Cited Findings

**EU AI Act: scope and obligations relevant to banks**
- Annex III, point 5(b) classes as high-risk AI systems used "to evaluate the creditworthiness of natural persons or establish their credit score", with an explicit exception for AI used to detect financial fraud — [Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)
- Under Art. 6(3), an Annex III system is not high-risk if it does not pose a significant risk, for example if it performs a narrow procedural task, improves the result of a completed human activity, detects decision patterns without replacing human assessment, or performs a preparatory task. A system that profiles natural persons is always high-risk — [AI Act, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)
- High-risk requirements: risk management (Art. 9), data governance (Art. 10), technical documentation (Art. 11), record-keeping/logging (Art. 12), transparency to deployers (Art. 13), human oversight (Art. 14), and accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity (Art. 15). Deployer duties are in Art. 26. A fundamental-rights impact assessment is required for deployers of Annex III 5(b) systems (Art. 27). Affected persons have a right to an explanation of individual decisions (Art. 86) — [AI Act, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)
- **EBA mapping (21 November 2025):** The EBA found "no significant contradictions" between the AI Act and EU banking and payments law and calls the Act "complementary". Most high-risk requirements are "fully aligned", "partially aligned" or "complementary" with sector rules. The EBA letter notes the Act gives no targeted derogations for other high-risk requirements such as human oversight, data governance and cybersecurity. The output feeds Commission guidelines under Art. 96(1)(e) — [EBA factsheet, AI Act implications for the EU banking sector (PDF)](https://www.eba.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2025-11/d8b999ce-a1d9-4964-9606-971bbc2aaf89/AI%20Act%20implications%20for%20the%20EU%20banking%20sector.pdf); [Vixio](https://www.vixio.com/insights/pc-no-structural-conflict-between-eus-ai-act-and-existing-regulation-eba); [A&O Shearman](https://finreg.aoshearman.com/eba-factsheet-on-implications-of-eu-ai-act-for-banking-and-payments-sector)

**EU AI Act: timeline status after the Digital Omnibus (as of late October 2026)**
- A provisional political agreement on the Digital Omnibus on AI was reached on 6 May 2026, and Member State representatives confirmed it on 13 May 2026. The agreed text **replaces the Commission's proposed conditional trigger (linked to standards availability) with fixed dates**: Annex III high-risk from 2 December 2027 and Annex I from 2 August 2028. National competent authorities remain competent for financial institutions. Art. 4 AI literacy is softened. The deadline for national AI sandboxes moves to 2 August 2027. Art. 50 transparency still applies from 2 August 2026, with a grace period to 2 December 2026 for Art. 50(2) watermarking of existing systems. The legal basis for processing special-category data for bias detection is extended — [Gibson Dunn, 27 May 2026](https://www.gibsondunn.com/eu-ai-act-omnibus-agreement-postponed-high-risk-deadlines-and-other-key-changes/)
- Formal act: **Regulation (EU) 2026/1744** (dated 8 July 2026), which entered into force on **27 July 2026**. Art. 113 now sets 2 December 2027 for Art. 6(2)/Annex III systems and 2 August 2028 for Art. 6(1)/Annex I. The new Art. 4 says providers and deployers "take measures to support the development of AI literacy" without guaranteeing a level. Art. 111(4) gives providers of systems on the market before 2 August 2026 until 2 December 2026 to comply with Art. 50(2) — [AI Act Explorer, Digital Omnibus](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/ai-act-explorer/digital-omnibus/)
- Search-result summaries add that the European Parliament approved the text on 16 June 2026 (423–57, 174 abstentions), the Council gave final sign-off on 29 June 2026, and publication in the Official Journal was on 24 July 2026 **[secondary, vote counts not verified against EP records]** — [CSA research note](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-eu-ai-act-omnibus-vii-deadline-delay-20260/); [Winston Taylor](https://www.winstontaylor.com/insights/ai-act-rules-on-high-risk-ai-delayed-as-ai-digital-omnibus-agreed). One Usercentrics page still says the Act "has not been formally delayed" while also giving the 2 December 2027 date, which looks like stale wording — [Usercentrics](https://usercentrics.com/knowledge-hub/eu-ai-act-high-risk-delay-article-50-transparency-consent/)
- Dates the Omnibus did not change: prohibitions and AI literacy have applied since 2 February 2025, and general-purpose AI model obligations since 2 August 2025 (the Omnibus left the GPAI date in place) — [AI Act, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj); [Gibson Dunn](https://www.gibsondunn.com/eu-ai-act-omnibus-agreement-postponed-high-risk-deadlines-and-other-key-changes/)

**GDPR Art. 22 and the CJEU**
- Art. 22 GDPR gives data subjects the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing that produces legal or similarly significant effects. The exceptions are contract necessity, Union or Member State law, and explicit consent, each with safeguards that include the right to human intervention, to express one's view and to contest the decision — [GDPR, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)
- **CJEU C-634/21 SCHUFA (7 December 2023):** Generating a credit score is itself an automated "decision" under Art. 22 when a third party "draws strongly" on it to decide whether to conclude, perform or end a contract. The Court rejected treating it as a merely preparatory act, which commentators read as a broad interpretation of Art. 22 — [A&O Shearman](https://www.aoshearman.com/en/insights/ao-shearman-on-data/cjeu-rules-that-a-credit-score-constitutes-automated-decision-making-under-the-gdpr); [Verfassungsblog](https://verfassungsblog.de/to-score-is-to-decide/)
- **CJEU C-203/22 Dun & Bradstreet Austria (27 February 2025):** Addresses the right to meaningful information about the logic of automated decisions (I did not verify the full holding) — [IAPP](https://iapp.org/news/a/key-takeaways-from-the-cjeus-recent-automated-decision-making-rulings). Germany's BGH reportedly ruled on four SCHUFA cases on 21 October 2026, tying the Art. 15(1)(h) access right to cases where Art. 22(1) applies **[single secondary report]** — [PPC Land](https://ppc.land/schufa-loses-blanket-trade-secret-defence-at-germanys-top-civil-court/)

**DORA (Regulation (EU) 2022/2554), applicable since 17 January 2025**
- DORA requires ICT risk management, major ICT incident reporting and ICT third-party risk management. That includes a register of information on all ICT third-party contractual arrangements, mandatory contractual provisions (stricter ones for ICT services supporting critical or important functions), concentration-risk assessment and exit strategies (Arts. 28–30) — [DORA, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2022/2554/oj)
- On 18 November 2025 the ESAs published the first list of designated critical ICT third-party providers (CTPPs). Secondary sources report 19 providers (hyperscale cloud, data centres, networks, financial-services technology), now under direct ESA oversight. They were identified using financial entities' registers of information, and one law firm says the list will be updated annually. Named examples in secondary sources include AWS, Google Cloud, Microsoft, Oracle, SAP and Deutsche Telekom (I did not verify the full list) — [Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/de/blogs/sourcingatmorganlewis/2025/11/dora-eu-regulators-announce-list-of-critical-ict-third-party-providers); [A&O Shearman](https://finreg.aoshearman.com/esas-publish-official-list-of-designated-critical-ctpps-under-dora); [Regulation Tomorrow](https://www.regulationtomorrow.com/2025/11/esas-publish-list-of-designated-ctpps/)

**BNR (National Bank of Romania) statements**
- In its 2025 Annual Report, the BNR lists cybersecurity and financial-innovation risk among moderate-level systemic risks and says its relevance has grown with digitalisation and wider use of AI-based solutions. It does not call AI a direct financial-stability threat — [BNR Annual Report 2025 (PDF)](https://www.bnr.ro/uploads/2025-6raportanual_documentpdf_545_1782459656.pdf); [Wall-Street.ro](https://www.wall-street.ro/articol/banking/ai-ul-folosit-de-banci-intra-pe-lista-riscurilor-urmarite-de-bnr.html)
- The BNR recommends stronger governance and cyber resilience as AI is adopted, with "rigorous vulnerability assessments" and adapted governance and internal processes — [Business24](https://business24.ro/guvernanta/consolidare-guvernanta-rezilente-cibernetice-sector-1659001)
- The BNR Financial Stability Report of December 2025 says widespread AI adoption in finance raises cyber and financial-stability risks alongside its benefits — [BNR FSR December 2025 (PDF)](https://www.bnr.ro/uploads/2025-12-09raportasuprastabilit%C4%83%C8%9Biifinanciare-decembrie2025_documentpdf_545_1765290572.pdf)
- In July 2026 the BNR responded to a European Systemic Risk Board (ESRB) warning on cyber risks from advanced AI models. It called the warning preventive and noted that its latest resilience exercise found no significant operational vulnerabilities — [Ziarul Financiar](https://www.zf.ro/banci-si-asigurari/bnr-clarifica-avertismentul-european-privind-inteligenta-artificiala-analiza-riscurilor-cibernetice-confundata-indemn-retragerea-depozitelor-renuntarea-serviciile-financiare-digitale-23202162); [Curs de Guvernare](https://cursdeguvernare.ro/bnr-avertisment-riscuri-cibernetice-test-rezilienta-bancneexistenta-vulnerabilitati.html)

### Inferences
- **Classification by process (my reading of Annex III and Art. 6(3); not legal advice):** reconciliation, payment E&I, popriri processing, complaint triage, trade-finance document checks and AML/sanctions alert triage are not listed in Annex III, so they are not high-risk under the AI Act. Fraud detection is explicitly carved out of 5(b). They remain subject to GDPR, DORA, AML law and supervisory model-risk expectations.
- **Loan application validation:** an agent that only checks completeness and consistency of documents and flags issues for an underwriter can plausibly rely on the Art. 6(3) "narrow procedural/preparatory task" derogation. If it scores, ranks or profiles retail applicants, it is high-risk from 2 December 2027 and engages GDPR Art. 22 and the SCHUFA ruling. The safest hackathon framing is "pre-check and summarise for the human underwriter, never decide credit".
- **DORA:** a third-party LLM API used in an operations agent is an ICT service. It belongs in the register of information, needs DORA-compliant contract terms (stricter if the process is a critical or important function, which payments operations likely are) and an exit strategy. Routing through EU-region hyperscaler endpoints (as RBI does with Azure OpenAI) puts the model behind a provider that is already under the ESAs' CTPP oversight. Under GDPR, EU-region processing also avoids Chapter V transfer issues.
- **Hackathon messaging:** "Designed to meet high-risk-style controls (Art. 12 logs, Art. 14 human oversight, Art. 15 accuracy testing) even where not legally required" matches the EBA's view that the AI Act complements existing banking governance, and the BNR's call for governance and cyber resilience.

### Gaps
- Not verified: the full official CTPP list, and whether any foundation-model provider (OpenAI, Anthropic, Mistral) is designated. I found no source confirming it.
- I found no BNR-specific guidance, circular or survey on GenAI or AI agents in Romanian banks. The BNR's Romanian statements are generic about risk.
- Not researched: Romania's designated national AI Act market-surveillance authority for banks. Under the Act, for financial institutions this is normally the financial supervisor.
- The C-203/22 holding and the EP vote count for the Omnibus were not verified against primary sources.
- Not covered: EU AML package (AMLR/AMLA) timelines that will change AML operations, and the EBA guidelines on loan origination (internal model governance for automated credit decisions).

## Q6. Failure modes of LLM agents in regulated operations, and recommended safeguards and architectures

### Takeaway
The documented failure modes are inaccurate extraction (ING's about 90% extraction accuracy still means roughly one field in ten needs checking), indirect prompt injection through documents (rated the top fintech LLM risk and described by the UK NCSC as possibly inherent), unverifiable or inconsistent decisions, automation errors that are fast to make and slow to reverse (e-Popriri), and projects cancelled for cost, value or control reasons (Gartner). The pattern that recurs in bank practice and in security research is: deterministic rules decide, the LLM extracts and drafts, confidence thresholds route cases, a human approves anything consequential, every step is logged, and actions are constrained by design.

### Cited Findings

**Failure modes**
- **Inaccuracy is the top practical problem:** In the BIS FSI survey (42 authorities, 2025), "inaccuracies in information provided" and user acceptance are the main challenges with GenAI — [BIS FSI Brief No 26](https://www.bis.org/fsi/fsibriefs26.pdf). ING's CDD extraction reached "nearly 90%" accuracy — [Evident](https://evidentinsights.com/insights/articles/ing-genai-in-wholesale-banking)
- **Indirect prompt injection via documents:** A 2026 fintech survey rates indirect prompt injection as the highest-priority risk in financial contexts because it reaches agents through retrieved documents without user interaction. It names KYC document parsing and RAG-based report summarisation as exposed workflows — [arXiv 2605.30650, "When AI Meets Wall Street"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.30650)
- The UK NCSC describes a scenario in which a malicious transaction in data analysed by an LLM could lead it to redirect a user's money, and warns that prompt injection may be an inherent issue with LLM technology — [NCSC, "Exercise caution when building off LLMs"](https://www.ncsc.gov.uk/pdfs/blog-post/exercise-caution-building-off-llms.pdf)
- **Agent-specific blind spots:** Filtering only prompts and outputs misses the intermediate steps where agents choose tools, fetch data and act, so the full agent trace should be monitored at runtime **[vendor view]** — [NHI Mgmt Group / Straiker, Jan 2026](https://nhimg.org/articles/ai-agents-in-banking-need-runtime-controls-beyond-appsec/)
- **Automation errors are fast, remediation is slow:** Automated garnishments block accounts within seconds while reversal needs multi-party human validation over days or weeks, with an accountability gap between bank, ANAF and bailiff — [JURIDICE.ro](https://www.juridice.ro/862896/interoperabilitatea-bazelor-de-date-in-procedura-popririi-electronice-de-la-automatizarea-interogarilor-la-riscul-erorilor-de-sistem.html). CNP-based matching can block protected income — [InfoContact](https://www.infocontact.ro/cont-blocat-abuziv-anaf-executor/)
- **Metric gaming in reconciliation:** High auto-match rates can hide a growing backlog of hard exceptions, so exception ageing and false-match rate must be tracked — [LearnSignal](https://learnsignal.com/blog/ai-for-account-reconciliations)
- **Judgment calls:** In trade finance, automation handles deterministic UCP 600 field checks but cannot adjudicate judgment calls — [FloridAI](https://floridai.agency/blog/letter-of-credit-discrepancy-rate-ai)
- **Programme-level failure:** Gartner expects more than 40% of agentic projects to be cancelled by end-2027 because of rising costs, unclear value or insufficient risk controls. It also warns about "agent washing" — [Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-06-25-gartner-predicts-over-40-percent-of-agentic-ai-projects-will-be-canceled-by-end-of-2027)
- **Explainability and contestability:** Credit scores can count as Art. 22 decisions (SCHUFA). Under the AI Act, high-risk systems need logs, human oversight and explanations of individual decisions (Arts. 12, 14, 86) — [A&O Shearman](https://www.aoshearman.com/en/insights/ao-shearman-on-data/cjeu-rules-that-a-credit-score-constitutes-automated-decision-making-under-the-gdpr); [AI Act, EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)
- **Cyber and outsourcing:** The EBA (Spring 2026 RAR), the ECB (Buch, 2026, secondary) and the BNR flag cyber and outsourcing risk from AI — [Crypto Briefing on EBA RAR](https://cryptobriefing.com/eba-frontier-ai-risks-banks/); [AI Pulse brief](https://buttondown.com/Horizonscan/archive/ai-pulse-daily-brief-2026-09-23/); [Business24 on BNR](https://business24.ro/guvernanta/consolidare-guvernanta-rezilente-cibernetice-sector-1659001)

**Safeguards and architectures documented in practice or research**
- **Constrain the agent rather than hope to detect attacks:** Beurer-Kellner et al. (June 2025; authors from IBM, EPFL, ETH Zurich, Google, Invariant Labs and others) argue that general-purpose agents are unlikely to give reliable safety guarantees with current LLMs. They propose six patterns: Action-Selector, Plan-Then-Execute, LLM Map-Reduce, Dual LLM, Code-Then-Execute (CaMeL-style) and Context-Minimization. Action-Selector gives the strongest guarantees against injection affecting actions — [arXiv 2506.08837](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.08837v3); [Simon Willison summary](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Jun/13/prompt-injection-design-patterns/)
- **Dual-LLM separation:** A privileged model that plans and calls tools never reads untrusted content directly, and a quarantined model processes the documents — [lord.technology summary of design patterns](https://lord.technology/2025/06/14/securing-llm-agents-against-prompt-injection-through-design-patterns.html)
- **ML or LLM proposes, deterministic rules decide:** In reconciliation, ML generates and ranks match candidates while deterministic rules keep the auto-match decision — [Digiqt](https://digiqt.com/ai-agent/financial-services/reconciliation/nostro-reconciliation-ai-agent-for-reconciliation-in-financial-services/)
- **Human in the loop as standard bank practice:** ING keeps a human in the loop for its CDD and KYC agents — [Evident](https://evidentinsights.com/insights/articles/ing-genai-in-wholesale-banking). Lloyds keeps GenAI internal until its guardrails are trusted — [PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/news/artificial-intelligence/2025/lloyds-bank-and-coinbase-inside-their-generative-ai-playbooks/). BNP Paribas limits Doc Factory to basic mortgage files — [BNP Paribas](https://group.bnpparibas/en/news/ai-at-bnp-paribas-3-using-artificial-intelligence-to-speed-up-granting-of-mortgage-loans). Alpha Bank's Dana hands complex cases to humans — [DRUID](https://www.druidai.com/news/druid-ai-and-alpha-bank-launches-dana-virtual-assistant)
- **Payments safety pattern for agent-initiated actions:** BT and Visa's agentic payment pilot kept existing rails and controls (SCA via passkey, tokenisation, real-time fraud monitoring) — [BT press release](https://www.bancatransilvania.ro/news/comunicate-de-presa/comert-agentic-in-timp-real-banca-transilvania-a-finalizat-cu-succes-prima-plata-initiata-prin-intermediul-unui-agent-ai-cu-visa)
- **Use structured data where it exists:** Free-text MT messages make up more than 72% of E&I communications (Swift via Bottomline), and ISO 20022 structure reduces ambiguity — [Bottomline](https://www.bottomline.com/resources/blog/iso-20022-enabled-customer-case-management-final-hurdle-overcome-achieving-payments-modernisation)

### Inferences
Recommended reference architecture for the hackathon agent. This is my synthesis of the sources above and maps to the jury's 25% "agent design and orchestration" criterion.
1. **Intake and classification:** Classify the incoming item (popriri order, payment investigation, alert, complaint) and route it to a case type. An LLM can classify, but routing to tools uses an allow-list (Action-Selector pattern).
2. **Extraction with a schema and per-field confidence:** The LLM extracts into a strict JSON schema (debtor CNP/CUI, amount, creditor, bailiff file number, legal basis, dates). Each field carries the source span (page and offset) for traceability, and a confidence score or validator flag. Document text is treated as data only; instructions found inside documents are never executed (Dual-LLM / Context-Minimization).
3. **Deterministic rules engine decides:** Codified bank procedures, the kind ARI already indexes at Raiffeisen, run as testable rules. For popriri: CNP match, account-type exclusions, protected-income limits, multi-bank amount reconciliation and priority ordering. For reconciliation: tolerance windows and many-to-one matches. The LLM never decides money movement or compliance outcomes on its own.
4. **Confidence thresholds and routing:** High confidence with all rules passing goes to straight-through processing with a post-hoc sample check. Medium confidence or any rule conflict goes to four-eyes human review with a pre-drafted rationale. Missing data triggers an agent-drafted request (a letter to the bailiff, an MT199/camt query) for human approval. Unexpected inputs (unreadable scan, unknown document type, contradictory data, suspected injection) are escalated with an explanation. Handling these well is what the jury will score.
5. **Tools with least privilege:** Read-only lookups (core-banking stub, customer master, sanctions list), "draft" write tools (response letter, ledger proposal), and no autonomous payment execution. Payment-like actions reuse existing approval and SCA controls, as in the BT/Visa pilot.
6. **Audit trail by default:** Log every prompt, tool call, rule result, model version, threshold and human decision. This supports AI Act Art. 12/14-style controls, GDPR contestability and DORA incident analysis.
7. **Evaluation set and KPIs:** Build a labelled set of 50–200 synthetic cases, including adversarial ones (an injected instruction inside a PDF, a wrong CNP, a protected allowance, a duplicate order). Report field-level precision and recall, rule-decision accuracy, STP rate, escalation rate, false-match rate, average handling time against a manual baseline, and cost per case. Run repeated trials to measure consistency (temperature 0, same inputs, same outputs).
8. **Deployment story:** An EU-region hosted model through a hyperscaler (the DORA register and CTPP oversight context), data minimisation (mask CNPs before LLM calls where possible), and a fallback to a manual queue if the model is unavailable (a DORA exit and continuity argument).

### Gaps
- I did not find a quantitative, independent study of LLM decision inconsistency (run-to-run variance) in bank operations. A benchmark claim that injection succeeds 20–50% of the time against simulated banking agents (AgentDojo) appeared only in a vendor blog and was not verified against the paper.
- No regulator has published a prescriptive agent architecture for banks. The architecture above is my synthesis, not a supervisory standard.
- I found no published accuracy, STP or error-rate data for any Romanian bank's document-AI deployment, including BCR popriri. Teams should treat any Romania-specific uplift as an assumption to validate with the bank.
